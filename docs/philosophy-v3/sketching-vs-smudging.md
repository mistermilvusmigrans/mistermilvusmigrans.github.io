# Core idea
A picture may be made of many colours; it does not make sense to smudge them into one hue, nor does it make the picture more sensible. Now, do the following substitutions to get the idea, before I lay the idea out more precisely:

| Picture | Colours (not exhaustive) |
| --- | --- |
| Character | Values, practices, deeds |
| Society | Culture(s), communities, institutions |
| Ecosystem | Climate, flora/fauna, dangers, opportunities |
| Structural integrity | Load-bearing capacity, resistance to various pressures |

The idea, laid out more precisely, is this:

> A multi-dimensional observation cannot logically be collapsed into a uni-dimensional evaluation unless the context sufficiently narrows the parameters of evaluation and/or the multiple dimensions can be combined while retaining relevant information for evaluation in a given context/range of contexts.

# What this means
Every measurement and metric is the product of selective focus. When you are evaluating something/someone in a given context via measurements or metrics, you should ensure you are doing justice to the context and to your purpose by not omitting what you need (e.g. omitting the motivations of a job applicant, instead looking only at qualifications) or combining what you cannot (e.g. adding the safety and cost-effectiveness of various choices for a vacation, in order to get the total "score" of the choice).

Furthermore, an evaluation may require the consideration of competing factors (e.g. the load-bearing capacity of a bridge vs. the cost-effectiveness, rigour in designing an application vs. profit-making margins relying on timely or even rapid deployment, etc.), which cannot necessarily be collapsed into a threshold-based conditional (e.g. "if X is greater than Y by q, then...") or a combined measure (e.g. weighted sum of scores). Just to be clear, my critique is about hidden or unexamined weights, not about weighting itself, since weights often encode preferences that may well be valid for a given context and purpose.

> This applies for qualitative and quantitative metrics.

The same principle applies for:

- Orientation toward a person
- Orientation toward a state of affairs
- The nature of a societal structure
- Attractiveness/beauty

This is not to say we cannot have a unified evaluation of something or someone in a given context, but rather, it is to say that such a unified evaluation has to be earned by context, purpose, and logic, and it is often NOT earned even when it is stated with confidence.

# What is it for
The core value of this approach can be analogised as follows:

> A machine works by mechanism, not by verdict.

In other words, by refusing to "smudge" various observations and considerations into a unified evaluation that is not warranted, we are keeping the facts and our orientation toward them clear in a multi-dimensional action-space, not narrowing our focus to a few dimensions while omitting the others. We are choosing to "sketch" rather than "smudge", thereby arriving at a clearer picture. Let me make the value of this clear with a few examples.

It is common to label people as "good" or "bad". A verdict of a "good person" or a "bad person" gestures at something inherent, something essential about a person that goes "deeper" than their actions and orientations. And then, we hear of a "good person" beating their child in anger, or a "bad person" fighting for a nation's freedom. Then, our categories are questioned, and the choice seems to be between relabelling the deed or relabelling the doer. Perhaps the victim would be blamed. Perhaps the perpetrator would be forgiven due to mitigating factors. Perhaps the freedom fighter would be demonised, their contributions diminished. And whom does this serve? Not the victim, not the beneficiaries. Perhaps it serves those in power, perhaps those who cling to a narrative, perhaps none. I have a suggestion: just do not judge people in a sweeping manner, acknowledging that in each one there are many potentials that can be actualised in many ways. Would that not be clearer, more actionable, less in need of mental gymnastics which, impressive as they may be, do not serve truth or value?

As another example, it is tempting to deride or derogate a system, such as a government, an organisation, a political system, an economic system, etc. It may even be right to do so, and in any case, there may be genuine, even non-negotiable grievances about these systems. A government that turns a blind eye to embezzlement. An organisation that disregards its employees' health and personal time. A political system that keeps tending toward gridlock. An economic system that keeps widening wealth inequality. But is it then right tear these down? How many revolutionaries took over only to kickstart an even bloodier era? How many theorists proposed systems in stark contrast to the systems they opposed, only for their own to turn out to be unsustainable? How do we balance the opportunities that arise from a fast-paced, industrious organisation with the need for personal time? My point is not to interrogate these systems, it is not to not think of breaking down the systems and/or remake new ones (doing so may be right), it is not even to justify their existence or urge you to "respect" them. My point is to a bit trite but relevant: think before you act, condemn, or praise.

# Many-to-one mapping
Decisions, however, require choices, and choices must eventually become concrete. Concretes are particulars: this and not that, here and not there, etc. If the goal of thinking is action, then thought, no matter how multi-dimension in its considerations, must map to specific, concrete actions, sometimes one action. Hire a candidate or not? Commit to a partnership or not? Oppose the system or not? And so on and so forth.

There are many methods of making decisions, including decisions in spaces where optimisation is multi-objective and where choices involve tradeoffs that do not cleanly align with our every interest. To name a few: pros-and-cons (blunt, but can be useful), satisficing, identifying the Pareto frontier, heuristics (especially for time-bound decisions), etc. But still, the question arises: what about the multi-dimensional considerations that we would be collapsing? Does the necessity for concrete choices not contradict whatever has been discussed thus far?

Firstly, a choice now is not one-and-done, usually. There are a whole host of choices, each addressing one or more dimensions of considerations. For example, when partnering with someone for life, yes, there is the choice to commit, but there are also countless choices about how to show up for your partner, how to align/realign your paths, how to communicate issues/concerns, etc. As another example, hiring a candidate may be followed by a period of observation, a progression in responsibilities/assignments, etc., that follow from the wider evaluation of the candidate, beyond the evaluation that helped decide whether to hire them or not.

Secondly, a concrete choice does not collapse reality into fewer features. If you choose to distance yourself from a toxic system, that is a choice that does not nullify the facts about this system, its history, the opportunities it does provide, its potential, etc. Even if your current choice engages with only a part of this reality, based on your own context and purpose(s), knowing this reality more widely allows you to return to or engage with this part in different ways. For example, you can leave a country that has turned tyrannical while garnering support for it outside, drawing on considerations of its people and their potential. You can pick a career while acknowledging other paths you could have taken, maybe circling back to them later or holding on to that knowledge for future considerations and counsel.

Thus, choosing is not the same as smudging. You can sketch a map and choose to go to only one place, but you know where to go, and where else you could be going in the future. Life is not a thought experiment that ends after a decision, at least not usually.

# The cost of sketching
No effort is free, and sketching often involves a greater effort - cognitively, often across time to built and maintain the discipline - than smudging, because you need to (1) pay more attention to multiple dimensions for longer, (2) consider multiple paths, even when you end up choosing one, and (3) keep re-evaluating your position with respect to the multi-dimensional sketch.

A key point to note here, hence, is that smudging is not automatically "bad" or "wrong", and may be the most feasible option in many contexts. For example, in a low-trust society, you are going to be wary of strangers by default, because that default is usually protective under the usual uncertainty we have about strangers. We also do not keep relitigating our conclusions about our career choices, passions, and partners, unless we see a good reason to, because if we have no good reason in front of us, by what cadence do we re-examine our lives?

Treating sketching as an automatic "good" risks engaging in arbitrary effort, i.e. effort that is applied untriggered by reason or importance (as an aside, valid triggers for deeper or more detailed sketching may be: stakes, irreversibility, cost imposed on others, and surprising evidence). I have discussed arbitrary effort (cognitive or otherwise) in depth in the following:

- [Effective Purposefulness, Effectiveness, Philosophy in Practice Philosophy V1](/docs/philosophy-v1/philosophy-in-practice/effectiveness/effective-purposefulness.md)
- [Effective Rationality, Effectiveness, Philosophy in Practice Philosophy V1](/docs/philosophy-v1/philosophy-in-practice/effectiveness/effective-rationality.md)
- [Rational Application of Volition, Rationality in Practice, Epistemology, Philosophy V1](/docs/philosophy-v1/epistemology/rationality-in-practice/4-rational-application-of-volition.md)

Hence, the key takeaway should not be "sketch, don't smudge", but rather, do not automatically collapse uncertainty into "smudged" certainty, and be honest about your smudges by acknowledging the context and your uncertainty. Despite the greater effort that sketching takes, it is important to build our sketches in whatever way we can afford to, bit by bit or diving deep, because a smudge does not smudge reality, only your view of it, and reality always has the final word in what happens next.

> **NOTE**: The justification for a smudge is not purely economical, of course; its economy is just a part of what justifies it. For example, if a smudge costs someone else more than it costs you (e.g. if you label someone as a liar based on a suspicion and refuse to trust them, the verdict may be economical for you but potentially damaging to them), it may not be earned (although it may be, because you are not obligated to absorb risk that you consider too high to ignore, even if it hurts others). Other considerations can also be made, e.g. whether your desire for the smudge is stronger than any link you can grasp to your values (e.g. the desire for revenge may make a vendetta feel justified, even if it is destructive for everyone involved in fact).
