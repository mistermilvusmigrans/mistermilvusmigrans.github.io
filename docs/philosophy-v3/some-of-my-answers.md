<h1>Some of my Answers</h1>

---

A lot has been written on philosophical questions, i.e. questions centered around that which pertains to the provenance of existence, knowledge, and values. Whole systems have been made, modified, and countered. I have partaken in such pursuits as well. But, perhaps, if I may be so arrogant, the answer is not as elusive as it may seem, given the history of our long, stumbling journey through fog and failure. I think this, because when it comes down to the basic facts on which we lay our foundations, the facts themselves are very straightforward, so straightforward they almost hit you in the face. Yes, "obviousness" is a poor argument, since so many "obvious" claims have been completely opposed to one another, so take my confidence about the "obviousness" of my ideas with a pinch of pepper, if you will.

One fact is that what is, is. And something is. I am. I function. My functions follow from what I am. My knowledge follows from what I perceive, and I also perceive the fallibility of my knowledge-seeking, having been wrong over and over about something or the other. My values follow from some goal, and ultimately, a goal has to be from something unchosen, since choice needs some context. Meaning emerges from values, and values emerge from the mechanisms that give context to my choices. No "meaning" exists "out there", but meaning does exist, or at least can.

This is the essence, to me, of metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics.

And yet there is more.

Let me take a sharp turn toward "god". There is no god that I know of, and if there were, existence would bind them as it binds me. But let's talk about god. There is no such thing. Or rather, "god" is a bad concept that carries the authority of some telos beyond humans and "above" humans in power and scope. It takes the shape of whatever is in the minds of those who wish to appeal to this authority. An all-encompassing benevolence to the benevolent, an all-encompassing judge to the judgemental, a protector to the fearful, an ally to the ambitious. God's attributes change, the place of their abode changes, their relationship with the world and the humans in it change, and all this changes, curiously, in tandem with changing ideas, practices, people, and circumstances. Sometimes there are many gods, sometimes one, sometimes none. The same scriptures produce debates spanning centuries, or an oppressive dogma that stamps out debate for centuries.

Now, anyone can interpret, reinterpret, and misinterpret that which exists. Stars as points of light stuck to the world's ceiling, animals as unfeeling automatons, storms as the wrath of spirits. But if anyone, from a paleolithic hunter-gatherer to an astrophysicist, were to describe a star, an animal, or a storm, they would be pointing to the same thing with a different perspective about what that thing "really is". But what are people pointing to when they describe "god"? There is something in common, but where? In the facts "out there" in the world? Evidently not; no one agrees on some shared vision or revelation. But what they do agree on is that a god has power and intention and somehow, this power and intention relates to you. The powers and intentions themselves may vary as much as humans vary, but somehow, the constant is that there is some power to be in relationship with, reckoned with as a consciousness out there rather than just the world doing what the world does.  In other words, what is common exists not in observed fact but in the features of a conscious agent projected out into systems that may not possess consciousness. Agency detection turned into a practices, rituals, cosmology, and philosophy.

And curiously, this consciousness seems to reflect the culture and the individuals in it, across different cultures and different individuals. Warlike gods such as Zeus and Odin for warlike cultures like the Ancient Greek and the Norse, patriarchal divinely-inspired hierarchy in patriarchal societies, philosophically sophisticated abstractions in more complex, older and environmentally stabler cultures such as those of ancient and medieval India, a god that just so happens to favour one's own group against the others like the Jews, a god that outlines guidelines for slave-upkeep in a culture where slavery was common, etc. Hence, it seems another commonality is the set of needs, fears, and desires that drive people to pray, worship, observe some teachings or make others observe them.

Hence, "god" is a bad concept because it blurs the human needs, fears, and desires that make people seek such authority in the first place. It externalises power and purpose in a way reminiscent of Plato's reification of the universals. What I am saying here is not simply the standard atheistic stance of "there is no god"; I am saying, rather, that "god" exists as an aspect of humans, which is incoherent, because gods are supposed to be "out there"; this is why, in my opinion, "god" is a bad concept. By recognising "god" (insofar as we can define this concept at least somewhat coherently) as a part of humans, I think we can put an end to this maddening project of finding "purpose" in the world's workings, "meaning" in our place in this world, "value" as something written into reality itself. It is maddening, because it is such a waste of our one and only life (as far as we know) to pursue a phantom.

Now, let's talk about the infamous "is-ought" gap, which shall somewhat temper the self-assurance of the above paragraphs. I have brushed aside the external "objective" source of values, and I have planted my flag firmly in the soil of the naturalists, when it comes to values. This is where I would probably lose many of you, even those who have been on board up until now, but here it goes. Nothing matters from some neutral perspective. To be precise, nothing matters from nowhere in particular, only from a person's own perspective. This is almost a banal statement, but is worth saying, due to how easily this banality is hidden under high-sounding phrases. As such, whether you choose the path of a violent psychopath, or take the road of benevolence... whether you choose to devote yourself to discovery, or surrender yourself to mystery... nothing matters from some neutral perspective, not because they are "equal" options, but because, in a view from nowhere, they are not options at all, just things that happen as they happen. Whether you die in the presence of those who love you, or die alone in your own puke, it is all a self-sustaining system that failed to sustain itself in a world not purpose-built for its sustenance. Yet, something does matter, because these options are not the same to the one who may go through them in 1st-person.

There is no "ought" that binds you any more than the laws of physics bind you to make a bridge. That being said, if you do choose to make a bridge, the laws of physics cannot be negotiated with. And that, really, is the core of "ethics": a choice, based on something unchosen somewhere, that then shapes what "ought" to be done, not by some universal code of conduct, but simply by the unyielding nature of identity and causality. I affirm life, lived a certain way. I do not condemn those who oppose my way as if it were an affront to what is capital-R "Right" in and of itself. If I condemn them at all, it is simply because they are against me and my values. This is not a trivial thing to me, but I do not claim that the universe itself demands their punishment.

Now, what must be done about disagreement? There are a lot of good tools here. Discussion, debate, tolerance as long as it stays coherent (an incoherent form of tolerance would be tolerating views that are not themselves contradictory to coexistence), systems of consent... or, more distastefully, systems of control, conquest, battle and bloodshed. I know what I prefer, and it is important for me that I try making others see my way enough that coexistence is possible and potentially fruitful.
