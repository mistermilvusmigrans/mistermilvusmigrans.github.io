# Baseline
Here is a tension in my thoughts about life: (1) I think life as such has no meaning, that everything is essentially meaningless, and yet, (2) I think that value emerges from life as lived concretely, and value shapes meaning. (1) and (2) together sound like a contradiction, and they are a contradiction until you clarify the semantics of the word "value". When I use the word "value", I am speaking of it in the basic, almost mechanistic sense of "that which one acts to gain and/or keep" (which is how the novelist-philosopher Ayn Rand defined "value"). It is a description, and it is only a prescription insofar as you choose to consciously pursue it as a part of a broader pursuit of value. Since I think that the root of value is fundamentally unchosen (see ["Root" in this document](#root)), I believe values are fundamentally descriptive, being prescriptive only in more specific, scoped contexts.

Now, when the word "value" is used in general, it is (in my understanding) often used as primarily a prescription. In this view, even when you *describe* something as "valuable" or speak of "value" as something you can describe, you essentially prescribe the evaluation of the object of value, i.e. you prescribe that something should be held with a certain level of importance. By extension, when you speak of "values" in life, you prescribe the pursuits or achievements one must do or strive toward, as something intrinsic to life, an objective standard rather than something conditional on some unchosen goal-driven foundation.

I believe this is the discrepancy that leads to the contradiction I spoke of. To me, value is primarily descriptive, with prescriptions being conditional and perhaps even hypothetical, more the recognition of or assumptions about the requirements for success of a certain kind, rather than requirements for moral choice as such. In my view, not abiding by a value is no sin or crime or "violation" in a moral sense, it is simply practice that does not meet certain requirements for success of a certain kind, and nothing makes this success intrinsically valuable. It is as valuable as we choose it to be, or as valuable as our nature shapes it to be... or both.

The question then arises: does this not diminish moral seriousness? In a sense, it does, i.e. in the sense that it makes morality more of a tool or a technology than a non-negotiable requirement for validating one's actions and attributes. However, I think if you are here and choose to remain here in this world in a certain way, the seriousness emerges from the logic of your choice and its demands, in the same way corollaries emerge from a theorem, or in the same way a system's architecture emerge from a client's needs/desires.

Coming back to the initial point, everything is essentially meaningless when speaking of "value" as spoken in the general moralistic/quasi-moralistic sense, but meaning does exist, emerging from value when speaking of "value" as spoken in a mechanistic/architectural sense. And in this latter sense, all you lose, I think, is metaphysical teleology, not some deep-seated human need.

# Root
Let us now turn to this "unchosen" foundation I keep referring to. What the hell is it? Even if I did not know, I cannot but accept its existence in some form due to the logic of valuing itself, in that values are hierarchical, and a choice-based hierarchy of values cannot be based on choice, since choice requires some basis on which to make choices (if this basis is itself a choice, that choice is based on an arbitrary decision, which is practically no different from an unchosen basis in the sense that it has no basis for "what for", just a basis for "is"; and if it did have a basis for "what for", this basis is outside this choice and thus unchosen by agent in question). For a deeper elaboration of this point, see: [Ethics, Philosophy V3](./ethics.md).

But I think I do know what this unchosen foundation is, speaking more generally and abstractly. It is that which you take for granted, plain and simple. You can keep digging deeper (and there are many ways to dig or "interrogate"), take fewer and more fundamental positions for granted, but ultimately, you have to take something for granted, i.e. something you take as a given, which you cannot or will not interrogate in terms of "choice" (i.e. in terms of "what for"). Perhaps if you are psychologically "healthy" (in some definitions, although these definitions may be more about "normal" than "healthy"), this foundation would be the will to life. To others yet, this foundation could be the will to power, for which life is the means. If you yearn for autonomy, perhaps this foundation would be to live life unfettered by arbitrary impulsions and impositions, even if it costs you your very life. If you yearn for security and stability, perhaps this foundation could be loyalty to your tribe, to your kith and kin.

In the end, I do not know what this foundation is for you. I only know it exists, I know it can be interrogated in many ways, but only to a point; there is no infinite regress here, because you cannot ask infinite questions. And if you choose to ask questions indefinitely, I suppose you must have taken for granted that this pursuit for answers is itself is worth doing.

For better or for worse, this view of values is not safe, i.e. it has no in-built guardrails, it does not promote some shared vision of goodness, it does not even give you the foundation of morality to begin with. Cruelty and kindness, cynicism and openness, mysticism and rationality... these are on the table, perhaps not on your table, but on someone's table, possibly. I can only say that my foundation is what it is, and I take it to be a matter of life and death (by mechanism (since life draws its value from this foundation), reinforced by affect and lived experience), and I shall strive to shape my life and the world by it. I am not universally right, I am just alive and seriously opinionated.

# Stem
When I spoke of "taking for granted" above, I collapsed two ideas into phrase: (1) something that cannot be interrogated as a matter of choice because it emerges from unchosen facts, and (2) something that simply IS NOT interrogated for whatever reason. This, on some level, gives the same validity to the foundations of someone who takes their love of mathematics for granted, of someone who commits to a dogma without any skepticism for it, of someone who has interrogated their nature and discovered their drive to life and joy, of someone who has confronted death and decided that they are oriented toward life after all.

I posit this: the validity of their foundations, under my view, *is the same*, when we speak of "moral" validity, precisely because there is no moral validity to speak of here; moral validity comes up once the foundation is set, not before. The purpose of scrutiny, hence, becomes this: are you willing to settle, or are you willing to dig deeper? My guess is that this willingness is kind of an orientation in a sense, perhaps rooted in some orientation toward truth and a respect for reality that may itself be unchosen. But this is just a guess. Nonetheless, someone could also unwillingly confront their foundation, especially when it fails to fulfil them in some way. Either way, whether done willingly or unwillingly, the purpose of scrutiny becomes not to invalidate one's foundation but to discover a foundation that stands the test of the interrogation. The interrogation itself could vary in detail AND in kind: the interrogation could be philosophical, introspective, extrospective, theological, intuition-driven, exploration-based, etc. All I am pointing to is that it may be worth looking deeper if you want to look, for there may be more layers to uncover behind what you take for granted.

> **NOTE: Invaliding vs. revealing**: I said above that the interrogation does not invalidate your foundation but actually discovers it. The reason it does not invalidate it is the same reason the various foundations I referred to were of the same "moral" validity: they have no inherent moral validity. Moral validity does not even exist before the foundation, but rather, emerges from it.

> **NOTE: The kind of interrogation**: The kind of interrogation matters, and just because your foundation survives one kind does not mean it will stand the test of other kinds that may come up willingly or unwillingly. You can philosophically convince yourself to a life-affirming, productivity-driven philosophy while finding yourself drained and nihilistic by the demands of the very same philosophy. You can dig deeper into your faith and become disillusioned by it intellectually, only to return to your faith seeking community and spirituality. I am not prescribing pathways here, I am just pointing out that, detached from a more concrete context, there is no fundamental floor to this foundation.
