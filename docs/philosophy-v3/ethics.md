# Context
The way I understand it, every discipline is founded on a question, an instigating problem. For ethics, the question is this: how to choose? Not in a specific situation, or a specific range of situations, or specific domain or area of life or whatever, but in general? On what basis is any choice made? Whence comes this question? It comes from understanding that every choice is either an end in itself or a means to some other end, and by analysis, I posit that most of what we do as living, conscious, volitional beings is not an end in itself. I even posit, drawing from my argument in [The Standard of Value, Ethics, Philosophy V2](/docs/philosophy-v1/ethics/1-standard-of-value.md), that our purposes trace back to the purpose that is the sustenance of our life, lived by the shape of some unchosen foundation (as I have argued in ["Provenance of value", Foundations, Philosophy V3](./foundations.md#provenance-of-value)).

Based on these propositions, the question becomes:

***How to choose so as to attend to the core of my values?***

> Since this core extends outward into everything we choose to do, this basis becomes the basis of everything we choose, be it explicitly or implicitly.

# No embodied moral goodness
To be embodied is to be present as the part of the identity of an entity. An entity is something that exists concretely and independently (i.e. whose existence is not as an aspect or attribute alone). Moral goodness, I think, refers to "worth" as per some ethical standard (ethics being the study of the foundations of choice). Drawing from the definition of "worth" in [`working-results/worth-applied-to-the-self.md`, `the-way-of-the-cartographer` (Philosophy V2), **github.com**](https://github.com/mistermilvusmigrans/the-way-of-the-cartographer/blob/main/working-results/worth-applied-to-the-self.md) as: "the objective standing of the systems that compose it, together with the quality of integration between them." In particular, this document defines "worth" for a self as follows:

> Worth, for a self, is the objective standing of the systems that compose it, together with the quality of integration between them - not a subjective wish, but a recognition of real potential for health, cognitive-psychological capacity, and agency, where agency is the successful integration of the other systems into a single capacity to act on one's own judgement, rather than a third system standing alongside them.

Embodied moral goodness, in this view, refers to the concrete facts that themselves refer to potential toward the good, not the good in and of itself. Why? Because moral goodness refers to "worth" from an ethical standpoint, and ethics concerns with choices, i.e. voluntary **action**, not a state of being, and, finally, "worth" for the self concerns with agentic capacity, which - from an ethical standpoint - would mean the agentic capacity to abide by ethical principles. In short, embodied moral goodness is not about being good, but having a suitable or even robust setup for doing good.

Now, in the heading of this section, I have rejected embodied moral goodness, which is strange considering how carefully I have defined it above (or tried to, at least). What is my point here? My point is simply that "embodied moral goodness" is, at least as defined, a contradiction in terms. Moral goodness can be enacted, but not embodied, and any attempt to "embody" it results not in actuality but in potentiality. You are not a "good person" due to your attributes but due to the actions you consistently and reliably do.

Now, have I engaged in an elaborate bit of wordplay? I mean, I have defined my term myself and then debunked it myself, almost as if I am playing chess with myself and naming myself a winner and a loser at once. But there is a point to this. Every term I have defined - "embodied", "moral goodness", "worth" - are defined (to the best of my abilities) to reflect real ideas within my understanding of ethics. When we talk about "embodied", we talk about qualities within a "body", a physical, *tangible* entity. When we talk about "moral goodness", we talk about achieving some ethical standard, and ethics does boil down, in my opinion, to the study of the foundations of choice. When we talk about "worth" with respect to our "self", we are talking about the potential of our "self" to act with goodness (even if we mean to say we would inevitably act with goodness). Thus, I do believe my definitions, which may not be standard, are nonetheless relevant to ideas on morality. At the very least, even if this section does not debunk "embodied moral goodness" as such, it does put it firmly outside the validity of the framework I am building.

> In conclusion: moral goodness is enacted, never embodied.
>
> **NOTE**: This conclusion holds *within* this framework.

# The point of virtue
Given that values emerge from the nature of an active system, and given that choices emerge from these core values, the point of virtue (i.e. categories of action that intentionally attend to one or more values) is purely instrumental. This is the same idea expressed in ["The instrumentality of virtue", Foundations, Philosophy V3](./foundations.md#the-instrumentality-of-virtue), but with more theory grounding it. Ethics is technology rather than aspiration or commandment. "Striving to be good" on its own is an incoherent goal; what matters is striving FOR the good, and "being good" is instrumental to it, if at all.

If virtue is purely instrumental, this opens the doors to the idea that what is "moral" depends on the context. Honesty may be great idea with a friend you know and love, and a horrible idea for boss who would load you with more work if they could or judge you for taking off when you have paid off-days left. Generosity usually strengthens goodwill among people, but it can, in the right (or rather, wrong) circumstances, waste your resources and/or merely cater to the entitlement among the recipients of your generosity. Justice is vital for building a high-trust society, but it arguably impracticable when bombing the lands of an aggressor nation in war to defend your own people, thereby taking (or potentially taking) many innocent lives.

However, if there is foundation for choice, there could be foundational virtues, i.e. key categories of actions that are broad enough to attend to core values, and thereby broad enough to cover all contexts. I think there certainly are foundational virtues. As I have argued in [Values and Virtues, Ethics, Philosophy V1](https://mistermilvusmigrans.github.io/philosophy-v1/ethics/2-values-and-virtues.html), rationality is one such virtue, although what it means in practice needs much more clarity than saying "act with reason" (see: [Rationality in Practice, Epistemology, Philosophy V1](https://mistermilvusmigrans.github.io/philosophy-v1/epistemology/rationality-in-practice/)). I am tempted to say this is the only foundational virtue, but I have no proof of that. I used to believe there were more, but I am not convinced of that either.

I suppose there is one more foundational virtue, one that I had never quite considered at this level before: the virtue of keeping one's mind sharp and heart open. I have no name for this virtue, so let's call it MSHO (mind sharp, heart open) for now. Here is what it consists of. Keeping one's mind sharp extends from rationality, but it also extends from the consistent practice of engaging with the world. The second clause emerges from my ruminations in ["Meaning and worth", Foundations, Philosophy V3](./foundations.md). Keeping one's heart open is the other side of engagement, i.e. the side that engages with concrete experience, not just cognitively or even sensorily but, you could say, with affect - emotions, impacts, reflexes.

And yet another foundational virtue I propose (also another virtue I never really thought of at this level) is: the virtue of empowering oneself through engagement (EtE). This follows from ["Engagement and empowerment", Foundations, Philosophy V3](./foundations.md#engagement-and-empowerment), and consists of valuing the power to engage and seeking to sustain it through engagement, either proactively (without hampering engagement) or in the moment. I think this virtue is important, as it emphasises that we are embodied beings who must act and be able to act to achieve our ends, and that valuing and sustaining this capacity is crucial.
