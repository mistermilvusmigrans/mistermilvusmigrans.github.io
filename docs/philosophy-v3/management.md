# Managing emotions through life
Emotions, in my understanding, are outputs of our system. They are are often an instantaneous evaluation of your internalised premises, internalised values, and the way these interact with the context you find yourself in. Desire for X arises when you hold X to be valuable and have the hope of achieving X. Fear of Y arises when you hold Y or its consequences to be dangerous to your values. Anger for Z arises when you hold Z to be opposed/harmful to your values in a way you can fight back against.

Joy, shame, guilt, anxiety, calm... these are results, results that can feed back into your system, but results of your system, primarily. Joy is a response that affirms the values your find in your life right now, shame is a response to your view of yourself as unworthy due to what you are or what you have done, guilt is a response to your view of your actions as unworthy and needing correction/atonement, anxiety is fear with no tangible target to latch on to, calm is when you are ready but not reactive just yet.

Internalised premises and internalised values need not be the ones you consciously hold or profess. This is something I have observed, at least, if you extend the definition of "internalisation" to something you have absorbed subconsciously, unconsciously, even physically, observable through implications (e.g. a physiological response that maps to a kind of evaluation in practice (e.g. disgust as an evaluation of something as "unclean", whether or not it "really" is), a subconscious bias you can observe as a tendency (e.g. a bias toward trusting people who look like you, even if their trustworthiness is not established), etc.).

But emotion are not just outputs of such an interaction, although many are. Emotions, we see, are also outputs of other interactions that may not be primarily based on premises and values, but on a mechanism apart from awareness. Biology, chemistry, and the various interactions between our systems and subsystems. Hormones can drop or fluctuate and make you feel less tolerant or patient. The lack of sunlight can make you feel depressed. A brief burst of activity can liven up your mood. The right foods can make you feel hopeful. Warmth or an embrace from a loved one can comfort or console you even if your situation has not really changed. The lack of sleep can bring despair to your mind, though nothing about your life has really shifted since when you were well-rested.

The upshot of this whole discussion is to point to one common thread: emotions are outputs, primarily. They feed back into our system and interact with it in many ways, but when we feel what we feel, that in itself is an output, a result. And here, we come to the idea of "managing emotions". In the light of the discussion so far, managing emotions is like managing symptoms, treating the output as the focus rather than the system and conditions and circumstances that created the output.

But here is another thought. What if emotions are NOT symptoms at all? What if this is, in general, the wrong analogy (because that note that it is an analogy, since a "symptom" is a concept that presupposes an issue). Is grief a symptom? Anger? Hatred? Or these simply outputs, that must be treated as such without pathologising them? Because in reason, I do not see why we should pathologise them. I grant that managing emotions head-on is often useful when we observe their effects to be immediately impactful to your self-control or composure or abilities. I also grant that certain emotions, especially painful ones, are strong signals that something in us or in our lives needs to be paid attention to.

But by themselves, unless you have reason to think of them that way, emotions are not "symptoms" or even information that must be processed, but affect, the bare consequence of you being alive and aware. If you want to treat them as information, then they are inded information (or at least carry it), like logs of a system that can be investigated, but need not be at every given point. I consider the treating of emotions as automatically worthy of examination (even painful ones) to be arbitrary effort unless you see the relevance in your context. Reflection is valuable, as is being thoughtful about your life and your choices, but by default, just let yourself feel what you feel. Reflect on it, now or later, if you want to, if you can afford to, but do not obliged to just by the fact that those emotions exist.

> Focus on managing your inputs, not primarily the outputs, although you may need to do the latter at times. And investigation needs a warrant; the existence of information is not by itself a warrant.

The key approach, to me, thus, is:

- Making regular space for reflection, i.e."
  - Whether my choices reflects my values
  - Whether my way of life meets my needs
- Organising my life by my values
- Organising my life by the needs of my:
  - Body
  - Psyche
  - Cognition
  - Relationships
- Committing to choices, not perpetually re-auditing them
- Investigating only with warrant

> **NOTE**: Regular reflection is about your life ("are my choices still serving my values?"), while warranted investigation is about a particular emotion/situation that you see a strong reason to delve deeper into.

# Managing life through structure
Have you tried keeping your focus on a unstructured all of text? A mish-mash of visual cues? A chaotic environment? An infodump with no explicit ties to core concepts and relevance, internally or externally? Free time with no plans and a lot of potential? A free space for ideation with no clear requirements or constraints?

My thesis is this: structure is the form that gives material its shape. Without structure, material does not become anything except what it is - material. Structure is the concretisation of identity. Now, when intention plays a part in the structuring, then structure is what turns material from "raw" to "used"; structure, here, becomes the concretisation of purpose.

Structure, to define it simply, is the set of attributes of X that together (1) relate parts of X to each other, and (2) relate X to things apart from X. A wholly thing that has no interrelated parts and is not relatable to anything else is undifferentiated, unintegrated, unidentifiable. To the extent that a thing is unstructured, to that extent it is undifferentiated, unintegrated, unidentifiable.

An animal's behaviour cannot be recognised without relating it to stimuli, to its environment, and to its own attributes. The nature of vertical movement on Earth cannot be recognised without relating objects that move vertically along with their measurements. Likewise, values, threats, dangers, and opportunities cannot be recognised in a life that does not relate values to consistent actions, threats to signals, dangers to evidence, and opportunities to values, threats, dangers, and the environment at large.

To structure one's life is not a luxury. It is a core part (though not the only part) of what living consists of, fundamentally, and to the extent that your life is unstructured, to that extent your life has no way through or no way forward. And here, it is important to de-emphasise structure a bit, because structure is actualisation, whereas potential is not strictly structured (if it were, it would be determined). Hence, leaving space for the unstructured is crucial.

Importantly, structure is not inherently "good", and not all structures rigid or pre-defined. Many of the strongest ones are indeed neither rigid nor pre-defined. "Unstructured free time" (if used well), "unstructured time with friends" (if enjoyed well), and so on, are not quite unstructured but dynamically and flexibly structured. Pursuing your desires without an overarching plan is not necessarily unstructured; it is unstructured in potential, actualising more and more of the potential as you recognise it, a structure that reveals itself over time, as you and your judgement grow.

> The key point is this: structure can actualise potential, but potential should not always be actualised immediately (if at all). When structuring your life, the goal is not to lay out a map of everything you are doing, can do, and ought to do. The goal is to actualise the potential with reference to your values, to the extent that you actually know or are willing to venture. ***The purpose of structure is to help you actualise your potential, not to ensure all your potential is immediately clear and actualised.***
