# What this document is about
My concept of an association between two things here refers to the assertion that there is some logical or causal relationship that implicates the presence/effect of one thing based on the presence/effect of the other. It is key note that "logical" and "causal" are separate, both in mechanism and in effect. An example, there may be no logical relationship between familial bonds and emotional bonds, i.e. the categories are separate, conceptually and potentially in practice, but causally (as we see in practice), these aspects (familial and emotional) are clustered. Finding a logical association grounds it in a non-contradictory structure, which is a powerful proof of the association if it exists, but not finding one does not mean make the association invalid or irrelevant.

An example of a logical association: consent and mutually-respectful relationships (at least considering the former as a pre-condition of the latter); omitting the former makes the latter incoherent and impossible. An example of a causal association: the association between prolonged comfort and complacency may be that prolonged comfort leads to the physical and psychological conditions that make complacency less costly and thereby easier to slip into due to the lack of clear/strong feedback.

A key point to note here is that real-life associations may not be logical or causal in underlying fact, only in presumption, assertion, or within a structure. For example, social conditioning may make people associate marriage with stability and the lack of it with "incompleteness", even if there is no inherent logical/causal association here. This is important, because when I question associations, I am not asking whether such associations exist psychologically or socially, but whether the assertions of these associations are actually true in a logical/causal sense. If not, these associations are, in my view, unfounded.

Another important point to note is that associations may become self-fulfilling or structurally/institutionally reinforced, even if there is no "natural" association apart from self-fulfillment and structure/institution. For example, in many places where Abrahamic religions are prominent, religion is tied to community as a matter of structure; to be a part of the community, you must subscribe to beliefs. In such a case, religion is causally tied to community, but such an association is not inherent in humanity as such but in the specific structures humans built. As another example, if people are conditioned to associate "ideal femininity" with passive/submissive traits, the association between these traits and the fact of being a girl/woman is non-arbitrary (and thus not "unfounded" in every sense) but contingent, not necessitated by some so-called "nature of woman". These are not to be brushed aside; they are valid associations that have real consequences that are valuable to know and content with. But I shall be clarifying such cases, because I believe strongly in the distinction between the human-made (intentional, designed/developed by or co-evolving with intentional beings) and the metaphysical (inherent in the nature of things in themselves, part-and-parcel of logic and causation); the former involves the questioning of intent, while the latter has no such consideration by itself. It is also relevant in order to know how to process an association and its implications; even if you decide a structurally-imposed association is worth following, the "worth" is tied to either practicality in the given context or the value of the structure itself.

This document focuses on untangling the unfounded associations (unfounded in my opinion) between various values, states of affect, virtues, motives/pursuits, and structures of life (social, interpersonal, or personal). I consider this to be an important exercise, since if an association is indeed unfounded, untangling its components would free me to make use of them in a configuration that works better for me, or to let go of the association entirely if it does not serve me, rather than feeling bound to it by necessity and nature. By understanding where an association emerges from, we can decide whether its components and their collaboration serve us or not, and if they, to what extent and in what context.

# [RANGE] Moral goodness
This is going to be surprisingly short, considering the depth of the topic of morality. But the essential point is this: there is no "good" that exists out of context, it is always "good" with respect to some standard, and it is up to us to decide whether a given standard serves us or not. For example, if you live with the association of moral goodness with obedience, dutifulness, or loyalty, even as it leads you to suppress your potential and power, be reliable without reward, or hold on to your side regardless of principles, it is worth questioning what good does "moral goodness" do for you. Likewise, if you live with the association of moral goodness with charity and selfless generosity, you must ask what standard do you adhere to and whom does it serve? Ultimately, it is worth asking: why are you adhering to the standards you adhere to? The promise of reward or justice after life? Urgency in the achievement of values here on earth? Harmony with society? Fear and/or weariness? An unquestioned norm?

> **NOTE**: Genealogy is not refutation, but it can raise suspicion.

> In conclusion:
> 
> - Moral goodness is contextual
> - Its standards are not above question

# [RANGE] Romance
## The logical case
Romance refers to a relationship of intimate emotional attachment/involvement. While it is often associated with sexual bonds when sexual attraction exists, it is not essentially associated in this way even theory, I believe. I suppose the non-sexual variant may be referred to as "Platonic", however, I believe romance as such covers both the sexual and non-sexual variants. And this is a key point: many things we associate with romance, such as attraction, intimate connection (physical and emotional, even sexual), and perhaps deep affection and admiration, are not exclusive to romance and can be found without it.

Another key detail we associate with romance is long-term pair-bonding. I think this association is relatively modern and not always widespread in practice (see the prevalence of arranged marriages, both currently and across history, which shows long-term pair-bonding without romance), and this points to a shift in the way we view the norms of long-term pair-bonding, rather than some inherent aspect of romance itself.

Exclusivity is something else often associated with romance: a one-to-one dynamic between people, with little-to-no room for anyone else on that level. While practical and psychological constraints may exist with respect to deep, intimate bonds, I think these cannot be generalised, and thus, cannot be tied inherently to romance.

Romance itself is a very specific framework for a relationship, and while it is often associated (especially in popular media or culture) as something "important" and/or "desirable" to anyone, I believe this association falls flat once we realise that humans have a variety of orientations in this regard (from aromantic to romantic within friendships to having aspects of romance without the whole "package" to sexually-intimate romance).

> In conclusion, romance can be one out of many frameworks for viable, sustainable, and fulfilling relationships (although many forms of romance are arguably not viable, sustainable, and/or fulfilling), but the evidence points to romance being "one of many desirable possibilities", rather than the ideal or most desirable one.

## The causal case
While any of the components of romance may not entail romance itself, is it plausible that they cluster due to causal factors? Yes, it is plausible. Particularly, it is very plausible (and often happens) that an intimate emotional + sexual bond is a very effective "glue" for long-term pair-bonding. Likewise, as I indicated before, the one-to-one dynamic in romance may well be due to practical and psychological constraints regarding deep, intimate bonds, making it logically not necessary to associate such a dynamic to romance, but practically plausible or even probably (although I am no expert on this).

> The point is to question, whether what you have with someone really necessitates the "package" that comes along with romance. Does a deep, affectionate bond with a person you are very attracted to NECESSITATE sexual consummation and a potential path to long-term pair-bonding (as assumed by some views of romance, though not all)? Can great value not be gained by a different - if more physically distant - configuration? How many potential friendships and found-familial bonds have been lost due to these expectations about how a relationship "should" progress. Again, one aspect can often lead to the other causally, but I am unsure how much of this connection is assumed due to conditioning, especially considering how fraught love-marriages can be for many people, especially young marriages, suggesting that other considerations (e.g. maturity, finances, practical partnership, etc.) may be essential besides these romantic aspects, perhaps even more essential.
